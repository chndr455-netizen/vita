00 / orientation

What are we trying to build?

VITA is a personal framework for preserving the capacity to live well, not a program for maximizing a health score. Its objective is to help you remain physically capable, mentally present and independent throughout your life.

Think of a long career as a marathon, not a succession of deadlines. The capacity to lift things, climb stairs, concentrate, enjoy family life and recover from demanding days matters in its own right. Health is not merely an input to productivity.

Lifespan

How long a person lives.

Healthspan

The years lived with good health and meaningful functional capacity. Definitions vary across studies.

Biological resilience

The ability to adapt to a challenge and regain function afterward.

Functional capacity

The ability to carry out daily activities, including movement, lifting and sustained effort.

The six pillars of VITA

01 · Sleep

Restore and regulate.

02 · Strength

Build and maintain muscle capacity.

03 · Cardio

Develop endurance and circulation.

04 · Nutrition

Supply the body with sufficient fuel and nutrients.

05 · Stress

Balance demands with opportunities to recover.

VITA is our proposed framework, not a validated clinical protocol, a commercial product, or an official NUS program. Start with the habits, not the devices.
01 / research literacy

What DELTA teaches us

The 2026 DELTA study followed one healthy participant who was also an investigator. Its research design combined lifestyle interventions with serial measurement of physiological responses, including responses to fasting. The core insight was to examine changes over time, rather than only isolated biomarker values.[1]

Why we must be careful

A single-person, unblinded study without a separate control group cannot establish broad effectiveness. Many habits predated the study, and concurrent changes complicate causal attribution. The authors caution against generalizing the regimen and disclose commercial and intellectual-property interests. Their experimental AI biological-age estimate is not proof of rejuvenation.[1]

02 / pillar one

Sleep: the foundation of recovery

Sleep is an active biological process, not simply an absence of work. It supports attention, memory, emotional regulation and physiological recovery. For adults aged 18–60, the CDC recommends at least seven hours regularly.[2]

Two systems influence sleep

Sleep pressure generally builds as we stay awake. Circadian rhythm is the body's roughly 24-hour timing system, influenced by light and daily routines. A consistent sleep schedule helps align these systems. Being exhausted does not necessarily mean falling asleep quickly if stress or timing interferes.

VITA practice

Morning · Wake at a fairly consistent time; get daylight when practical.
Daytime · Move regularly; avoid caffeine late in the day if it disrupts sleep.
Evening · Aim to finish demanding work before bed and allow a quiet wind-down.
Night · Reserve roughly eight hours for sleep, adjusting timing to fit your life.

Our sample schedule is 10 PM to 6 AM, with coffee ending around 2 PM and demanding work ending around 9 PM. See the VITA Operating Schedule for morning activity and evening wind-down. These are starting preferences, not physiological rules. Move bedtime gradually if needed.

Rule: Do not trade sleep for a workout. If the choice is 20 minutes of exercise or enough sleep, protect sleep and train later.

How to know if this is working

Review sleep duration, regularity and morning alertness together. A wearable's estimates of deep or REM sleep are less useful than persistent real-life symptoms; a consumer tracker alone cannot diagnose sleep disorders.[6] Persistent daytime sleepiness, loud snoring with breathing pauses or ongoing insomnia deserves medical evaluation.

Reflection

What currently affects your bedtime most: workload, commuting, phone use, caffeine, or something else? Pick one feasible adjustment.

03 / pillar two

Strength: protect your capacity

Muscle strength supports daily living, joint stability and physical independence. Resistance training gives muscles a reason to adapt. Improvement depends on repeated effort, adequate recovery and gradually increasing demands, not simply lifting the heaviest possible weight.

General guidance supports training major muscle groups at least twice weekly. Eight to twelve repetitions is a practical starting range, with gradual progression.[3][4]

Four principles to remember

Progressive overload

Increase the challenge slowly when the current work becomes comfortable.

Repetitions in reserve (RIR)

Finish a set feeling that you could still perform two or three controlled reps. No need to train to failure.

Technique

Control each movement, breathe normally and avoid forcing painful ranges.

Recovery

Allow rest between full-body sessions; avoid increasing weight when technique or recovery suffers.

Training for function, not appearance

VITA's strength pillar develops the capacity to lift, carry, climb stairs, maintain balance and live independently. Some muscular growth is a normal and useful adaptation, but physique, body weight, and muscle circumference are not our performance targets.

Past inactivity can mean less practice and conditioning, not a permanent limit on physical ability. Strength can improve through better motor coordination and muscle adaptation. We cannot infer the cause of previous weakness or current readiness from exercise history alone. Start at the level you have today, not the level you think you should have reached.

The 2026 ACSM position stand supports regular resistance training, including home-based exercise, for healthy adults. It emphasizes participation and working major muscle groups at least twice weekly rather than complicated programming. [9] The CDC recommends starting slowly and building up gradually. [10]

VITA · Workout 20M

Train for function, not appearance. Build for decades, not occasions.

3 days/week

Standing only

Equipment

Phase 0 · Optional re-entry (after a break)

You have used dumbbells and resistance bands since age 21, so this is optional rather than a mandatory beginner phase. If returning after a long break, start with two sessions per week (Monday A, Thursday B). Use bodyweight or the lightest securely assembled dumbbells for one set of 6–8 controlled repetitions per move (lunges: 4–6 per leg). Aim to feel that you could do at least 3–4 more repetitions. Skip optional holds if tired. A session of 10–15 minutes is enough; 20 minutes is a cap, not a quota.

Phase I · Establish the routine (start here if recovered)

If technique is familiar and you recover well, begin with the Monday–Wednesday–Friday schedule below. Aim for two sets per core exercise if time permits; use one set when returning after a break or under-recovered. Keep approximately 2–3 repetitions in reserve. No training to failure or maximum-weight testing.

Workout A — Full body strength

01 · Goblet squat · 1–2 × 8–12
Legs, glutes, trunk
Hold one dumbbell close to the chest, or start unloaded. Feet comfortably apart. Bend knees and hips, knees tracking with toes, then stand tall. Only descend as far as you can control.
02 · Wall push-up · 1–2 × 8–12
Chest, shoulders, triceps
Hands on a stable wall at chest height. Step back, keep body aligned, bend elbows and bring chest toward wall. Push away smoothly. To progress, use a fixed, sturdy countertop rather than an unstable chair.
03 · Bent-over dumbbell row · 1–2 × 8–12
Back, biceps, trunk
Soft knees, hinge at hips, keep spine neutral. Pull elbows toward waist, pause briefly and lower under control. Keep neck relaxed.
04 · Romanian deadlift · 1–2 × 8–12
Hamstrings, glutes, hip hinge
Hold dumbbells near thighs. With soft knees, push hips backward and keep weights close to legs. Stop when hamstrings feel tension without rounding the back; stand by bringing hips forward. No need to reach the floor.

Workout B — Strength and stability

01 · Reverse lunge · 1–2 × 6–8 per leg
Legs, glutes, balance
Step backward, bend both knees within a comfortable range, then return upright. Start without dumbbells; use a wall for balance. Substitute bodyweight squats if balance is difficult.
02 · Standing overhead press · 1–2 × 8–10
Shoulders, triceps
Start with very light weights at shoulder height, ribs relaxed and wrists aligned. Press upward without arching the back; lower slowly. Skip if overhead movement causes pain.
03 · Wall push-up · 1–2 × 8–12
Chest, shoulders, triceps

04 · Neutral-grip dumbbell row · 1–2 × 8–12
Back, biceps
As in Workout A, hinge at the hips and pull toward waist. Keep palms facing each other.
05 · Farmer's hold (optional) · 1 × 20–30 sec
Grip, forearms, trunk
Stand tall holding two light dumbbells at your sides. Keep shoulders relaxed, breathe normally and set weights down safely. Only do this if time and energy remain.

3 min: easy marching, arm circles, unloaded squats and hip hinges. Up to 15 min: strength work; rest 60–90 seconds or longer if needed. 2 min: easy movement and relaxed breathing. Finish the set safely and omit remaining sets rather than rushing. Breathe out during effort, in as you return; avoid prolonged breath-holding.

How to progress without chasing muscle size

First: learn controlled movement with a manageable load.
Next: increase reps within the prescribed range, keeping good form and reserve.
Then: after two comfortable sessions at the upper rep range, try the smallest secure weight increment and return to the lower rep range.
Maintain: if daily function and the program meet your goals, you do not have to increase weight indefinitely. Hold steady through busy weeks.

Progress one variable at a time. The actual dumbbell weight is unknown, so no kilogram target is prescribed.

Recovery decisions

Ready

Usual energy, no unusual symptoms, recovered from last session → normal planned session.

Under-recovered

Poor sleep, unusual fatigue or persistent mild soreness → reduce load or sets, walk gently or rest.

Stop

Sharp pain, faintness, chest pain, unusual shortness of breath or palpitations → stop exercise and seek appropriate medical assessment.

Long-term weakness

Unexplained persistent weakness, exercise intolerance, recurrent dizziness or a known medical restriction → seek medical advice before progressing.

Check locking collars on both ends of each dumbbell before every session; use balanced plates, secure footwear and a nonslip surface. If lying down is difficult because of pain, dizziness or breathlessness rather than space or preference, discuss it with a clinician. Seek urgent care for severe chest pain, fainting or severe breathing difficulty. This is a general home-training plan, not medical clearance.

04 / pillar three

Cardio: build endurance gradually

Aerobic exercise trains the heart, lungs and muscles to sustain movement. Brisk walking is enough to begin; there is no need to start with sprint intervals or expensive equipment.

The talk test

Easy

You can talk comfortably and sing.

Moderate

You can speak in sentences but singing becomes difficult.

Start with 15–20 minutes of brisk walking on three non-strength days. Add short walking breaks at work when practical. Choose a safe route, consider Jakarta's heat, rain and air quality, and use an indoor alternative on unsuitable days.

General adult guidance recommends 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity weekly (or 75 vigorous minutes), independent of muscle-strengthening sessions.[3] Our initial 45–60-minute walking plan is intentionally below this; increase through everyday movement as you adapt.

No need to exceed the 20-minute dedicated session limit. Three short walks plus additional brisk movement in daily life can gradually help close the gap.
05 / pillar four

Nutrition: fuel, not punishment

Food provides energy and building materials for muscle, hormones and tissue repair. Rather than copying DELTA's specific diet or supplements, VITA uses a varied, accessible pattern that fits normal Indonesian meals.

WHO emphasizes sufficient nutrition, diversity, balance and moderation, with more minimally processed plant foods and fewer foods high in excess salt, free sugars and unhealthy fats.[5]

Protein

Eggs, fish, chicken, tahu, tempe. Include a source with each main meal.

Carbohydrate

Rice, oats, potatoes, ubi. Adjust portions to hunger and activity; rice is not inherently bad.

Vegetables and fruit

Bayam, kangkung, carrots, papaya, bananas. Choose a variety.

Fats and hydration

Nuts, fish and unsaturated oils; water throughout the day, more when sweating.

A useful meal pattern

Illustrative plate guide, not an exact energy or nutrient prescription.

Example lunch: nasi, ikan or ayam, sayur bening or tumis vegetables, fruit and water. A mixed plate can still be nourishing even if the proportions vary. Choose more grilled, boiled or steamed foods when available and be mindful of sweet drinks and large amounts of salty sauces.

What about fasting?

A normal overnight gap between dinner and breakfast is an optional routine, not an essential intervention. Eat enough for work and exercise. A randomized trial in adults with obesity did not find a significant extra weight-loss benefit from adding an eating-time restriction to calorie restriction over 12 months.[7] This does not settle every question about meal timing, but it is a reason not to assume longer fasting is better.

A regular 7 AM breakfast and 7 PM dinner could work if comfortable; don't force it. Avoid extended fasting and supplement experiments. If you have diabetes, take medicines affected by food intake, have nutritional difficulties or a history of disordered eating, discuss meal timing with a clinician.

06 / pillar five

Stress: create space for recovery

Stress is not always harmful. A manageable challenge followed by recovery can support adaptation; persistent demands without recovery can leave you fatigued. Workload, sleep, family responsibilities and exercise all contribute to the overall burden. The concept of allostatic load describes cumulative physiological strain associated with repeated adaptation to demands; it cannot be diagnosed from a smartwatch score.

Build small boundaries

During concentrated accounting work, stand and move periodically. Take a real lunch break when possible. End your workday by writing tomorrow's priorities, then allow personal time. Prayer, conversation with family, a relaxed walk or a few quiet minutes may help you transition away from professional demands.

On difficult days: shorten training, take an easier walk or rest. Do not compensate for missed workouts with double sessions, reduced food or lost sleep.

A health routine should reduce strain, not become another source of anxiety. Persistent fatigue, mood changes or stress that significantly affects daily life deserves appropriate professional support.

Use a decision loop

01 · Observe: record a few consistent measurements.
02 · Interpret: compare a full week's pattern with your baseline and context.
03 · Adapt: change one manageable habit at a time.

A reassuring wearable reading does not rule out illness. Seek urgent medical evaluation for chest pain, fainting or severe/unusual shortness of breath; don't wait for a weekly review.
08 / implementation

The first 90 days

Our starting plan is deliberately modest. We will use a flexible 90-day observation and habit-building cycle, not a promise of measurable rejuvenation.

Days 1–14 · Baseline
Record sleep and energy, establish the morning routine and perform two or three standing strength sessions per week according to current conditioning. Use optional Phase 0 if returning after a break. Tag new habits; this is a practical starting reference, not an untreated research baseline.
Days 15–30 · Foundation
If recovered and comfortable, settle into three weekly strength sessions and three short walks. Protect bedtime. Use a regular meal pattern.
Days 31–60 · Development
Progress resistance or repetitions when ready. Add incidental movement. Make one affordable dietary improvement at a time.
Days 61–90 · Evaluation
Compare weekly trends, strength at equivalent form, perceived energy and how well the routine fits real life. Keep what helps.
